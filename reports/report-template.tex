\section{Machine Learning Pipeline}
\label{sec:pipeline}
% =====================================================================
The data comes from the DopNet dataset published by the UCL Radar Group \cite{dopnet}. It has been recorded using an Ancortek 24 GHz FMCW radar with a bandwidth of 750 MHz. The recorded samples are micro Doppler spectrograms that show the radar magnitude as a function of time and Doppler frequency. The Doppler frequency is proportional to the radial velocity of the measured moving object. Movement away from the radar generates a positive Doppler shift, while movement towards the radar generates a negative Doppler shift.
\\\\
The used dataset contains 960 labelled samples of different gestures made with a human hand. Each sample is provided as a CSV file, labelled with it's gesture, person and repetition. There are 4 gestures (wave, pinch, swipe and click) performed by 6 persons. The measurement for each combination has been repeated 40 times, resulting in a perfectly balanced dataset.
\\\\
Each sample has 800 Doppler bins, where zero Doppler is in the centre at bin 400. The dataset does not contain NaN or infinite values. The magnitude values have already been normalized to each sample's maximum at 0 dB. The goal of this project is to train a model that can classify the 4 gestures based on their spectrograms. The main model will focus on recognising gestures only from people it has seen before. The generalization of the chosen models to new people will also be tested, but that is not the primary objective for this report.

\subsection{Pre-processing}
Since there are no invalid values in the provided dataset, no samples have to be removed. All samples will be processed in four steps: noise removal, cropping, scaling and resizing. The goal of the pre-processing is to turn the raw data into clean spectrograms of uniform dimensions, that can be used for feature extraction and model training.

\paragraph{Noise removal}
As shown before in \autoref{fig:doppler_profile_persons}, the samples all have their own noise floor level. This background noise can be removed since it contains no useful information about the gesture. Because of the sample specific noise floor, the noise removal process also has to be sample specific. Clipping below a fixed threshold for example would cut into the gesture of some samples, while leaving noise in others. 
\\\\
The gesture mainly takes place in the bins close to zero Doppler, while the bins far away from the centre mainly consist of noise. \autoref{fig:doppler_profile_persons} shows the median levels off to a flat line. By inspection of that same figure, the outer 60 bins on each side are taken as the far bins. The median of the far bin pixels is then defined as the spectrogram's noise level. The median is well suited for this, rather than the mean, since it is less sensitive to a few bins that do contain the gesture (like the broadband stripes of the click gesture). As long as more than half of the far-bin pixels contain noise, the median gives a good estimate of the noise level.
\\\\
% \todo{add plot to show that it doesnt really matter what far bins we take}
Thresholding at the median would still pass half of the noise samples. Assuming complex Gaussian noise, the power per pixel $P$ is exponentially distributed with mean noise power $\mu$, so the probability that noise exceeds a threshold $p$ is 
\begin{equation}
  P_{\mathrm{fa}} = \Pr(P > p) = e^{-p/\mu}
  \;\Rightarrow\; p = \mu \ln(1/P_{\mathrm{fa}}),
\end{equation}
with the median at $p_{50} = \mu \ln 2$. The margin above the median is then independent of the unknown noise power and set only by the chosen false-alarm probability:
\begin{equation}
  \Delta_{\mathrm{dB}}
  = 10 \log_{10}\!\left(\frac{p}{p_{50}}\right)
  = 10 \log_{10}\!\left(\frac{\ln(1/P_{\mathrm{fa}})}{\ln 2}\right).
\end{equation}
As seen in \autoref{fig:noise_margin}, the far-bin pixels can indeed be modelled as Gaussian noise. The tail above 10 dB is explained by the broadband stripes in the click gesture. With $P_{\mathrm{fa}} = 10^{-3}$, i.e.\ the threshold at the 99.9th percentile of the noise, the margin becomes
\begin{equation}
  \Delta_{\mathrm{dB}}
  = 10 \log_{10}\!\left(\frac{\ln 1000}{\ln 2}\right)
  \approx 10.0~\text{dB}.
\end{equation}
This 99.9\% margin is a trade-off between removing more noise and preserving gesture information. For each sample, all values are clipped at the sample specific noise floor plus that fixed 10 dB margin.

\paragraph{Cropping}
Since the bins far from zero Doppler contain mainly noise and hold little gesture information, they might as well be removed to improve the Doppler resolution when resizing the spectrogram later on. For each Doppler bin, the pixels of all training samples are taken together, and the share that lies above the sample-specific noise threshold is computed (\autoref{fig:crop_rule}). This share decreases steadily away from zero Doppler. A bin is removed if less than 1\% of its pixels exceed the threshold. With a symmetric crop around zero Doppler, this keeps bins 90--709 (620 bins). A narrower crop would give a higher resolution after resizing, at the cost of removing the tails of gestures that do reach into the far bins. 

\paragraph{Scaling}
After the noise removal, each sample still has a different noise floor. The model should not learn the gestures based on this noise floor. The sample dependent noise floor should therefore be converted to a uniform noise floor. This is done using per sample scaling. The noise threshold (median + margin) becomes zero, since it has no information and the maximum (0 dB) becomes 1.

\paragraph{Resizing}
Finally, the spectrograms have to be made uniform in their dimensions so that they can be used as input for a CNN. A resolution of 128 x 128 is chosen as the output size. The spectrograms are all resized using linear interpolation. This does remove gesture duration, which did hold relevant information so that will be added as a separate feature later on.

\label{sec:features}
The CNN learns its own features from the resized spectrograms. The classical models, however, would have to work with $128 \times 128 = 16384$ pixels while only 768 training samples are available, and the resizing removes the gesture duration. The feature models therefore get a compact description of the gesture outline instead, based on the micro-Doppler envelopes of Amin et al.\ \cite{amin2019}. All features are computed on the spectrograms at their original length, so the duration is preserved.

\subsubsection{Envelope extraction}
\label{sec:envelopes}

\paragraph{Gesture power and gesture span}
The envelopes are computed on the gesture power $P(d, n)$: the linear power above the sample's own noise floor, using the same noise removal as in \autoref{sec:task1}, so that pure noise has power zero. Here $d$ is the Doppler bin relative to zero Doppler (positive is motion towards the radar) and $n$ the time bin. The Doppler axis is not cropped, since nothing is resized here. The paper uses 1-second windows that each contain one complete gesture. The DopNet recordings have different lengths with silence before and after the gesture, so only the gesture span is kept: from the first to the last time bin with at least 5\% of the power of the strongest time bin.

\paragraph{Step 1: effective bandwidth}
The spectrogram is split into a positive and a negative Doppler half. For each half the gesture power is summed over time and, counting outward from zero Doppler, the effective bandwidth ends where 99.9\% of the energy of that half is reached. This Doppler bin is the maximum positive Doppler $f_p$ or maximum negative Doppler $f_n$ of the gesture. The paper does not define the effective bandwidth; a fixed energy share is a common choice. Because the share is taken on linear power, a lobe 20~dB below the peak carries only about 1\% of the energy, so a share of 99\% would cut off the outer lobes of the gesture, while 99.9\% includes them. Isolated noise cells far from zero Doppler carry almost no energy and stay outside.

\paragraph{Steps 2 and 3: energy and threshold}
In every time bin the energy of each half is
\begin{equation}
  E_U(n) = \sum_{d > 0} P(d, n), \qquad E_L(n) = \sum_{d < 0} P(d, n),
\end{equation}
and the threshold is a fixed fraction of it, $T_U(n) = \sigma_U E_U(n)$ and $T_L(n) = \sigma_L E_L(n)$ with $\sigma < 1$. As in the paper, $\sigma$ is chosen per sample and per half such that the threshold lies exactly at the maximum Doppler in the time bin $n_p$ where that Doppler bin is strongest:
\begin{equation}
  \sigma_U = \frac{P(f_p, n_p)}{E_U(n_p)}, \qquad n_p = \arg\max_n P(f_p, n),
\end{equation}
and likewise $\sigma_L$ with $f_n$. In every other time bin the threshold scales with the energy of that time bin.

\paragraph{Step 4: envelope}
The envelope is the outermost Doppler bin that still reaches the threshold:
\begin{equation}
  e_U(n) = \max\{d > 0 : P(d, n) \geq T_U(n)\}, \qquad
  e_L(n) = \min\{d < 0 : P(d, n) \geq T_L(n)\}.
\end{equation}
Time bins without energy in a half get envelope 0.

\paragraph{Step 5: feature vector}
As in the paper, the envelopes are concatenated into one feature vector, $e = [e_U, e_L]$. The paper's samples all have the same length; the DopNet gesture spans range from 51 to 376 time bins. Each envelope is therefore placed in a fixed window of 358 time bins, starting at the gesture start and zero-padded after the gesture end. 99\% of the training gesture spans fit in this window; the 7 longer training samples (and 1 test sample) are cut. Nothing is resampled, so the gesture duration remains visible in the vector as the point where the zeros begin.

\autoref{fig:envelope_steps} shows all steps for one wave sample, with a maximum Doppler of $+282$ and $-269$ bins. The envelopes follow the swings of the wave: at the peak of each swing they reach the outer lobes, and between two swings, when the hand turns, they fall back towards zero Doppler. \autoref{fig:envelope_examples} shows the envelopes for one sample of each gesture. The extent of the envelopes differs clearly between the gestures: the pinch stays within about $\pm 60$ Doppler bins, while the wave swings much further out. The figure also shows the weak point of a threshold per time bin. In time bins with little gesture energy, such as a pause between two swings or the end of the gesture, $E(n)$ and therefore the threshold are so low that single noise cells far from zero Doppler pass it, and the envelope jumps (the wave around time bin 165 and the end of the swipe). The large excursion of the click is not noise but its short broadband stripe, which covers almost the whole Doppler axis.

\subsubsection{Available features}
\label{sec:feature_list}
From the envelopes of \autoref{sec:envelopes}, five groups of features are computed for every sample (\autoref{tab:features}). The first two groups are those of the paper; the other three are compact versions of the same envelopes, added for the classical models.

\paragraph{Window envelopes}
The feature vector $e = [e_U, e_L]$ of step 5, used by the paper as its main feature set. It keeps the full time resolution and the gesture duration, but it is large (716 values) compared to the 768 training samples, and two samples of the same gesture performed at a different speed do not line up in time.

\paragraph{Empirical features}
The paper also uses three empirical features as a baseline (Sec.~IV-A of \cite{amin2019}): the length of the gesture span $T = t_e - t_s$ in time bins, the ratio of the maximum Doppler frequencies $R = |f_p / f_n|$, which describes the balance between motion towards and away from the radar, and the Doppler bandwidth $B_w = |f_p| + |f_n|$.

\paragraph{Time-normalised envelopes}
Each envelope is divided into 16 equal segments of the gesture span, and every segment is replaced by its mean. Gestures of different speed then line up, while the length is kept separately in $T$. Averaging, rather than sampling single points, lets every time bin count and damps the single-bin jumps of the envelopes. With 16 segments, even the shortest gesture span (51 time bins) still has about three time bins per segment.

\paragraph{Envelope statistics}
Four statistics summarise each envelope magnitude, $|e_U(n)|$ and $|e_L(n)|$, over the gesture span: the mean, standard deviation and median describe its level and variation, and the 90th percentile is a robust maximum that a few jumps do not move.

\paragraph{Recording length}
The length of the whole recording in time bins, before the gesture span is cut out.

\section{Task 2: Model Development, Validation and Comparison}
\label{sec:task2}
% =====================================================================
\subsection{Model Selection and Design}
\label{sec:models}
Five models are tested on the pre-processed data of Task~1: three on the 27
hand-crafted features and two on the $128 \times 128$ spectrogram images.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Logistic regression (LR):} multinomial (softmax) model trained
        with the cross-entropy loss and an L2 penalty; the linear,
        interpretable baseline.
  \item \textbf{SVM (features):} soft-margin support vector machine; a linear,
        polynomial or RBF kernel allows a nonlinear decision boundary.
  \item \textbf{Random forest (RF):} decision trees, each trained on a bootstrap
        sample with a random subset of features per split, combined by
        majority vote; needs no scaling and captures feature interactions.
  \item \textbf{SVM + PCA (images):} PCA with whitening reduces the 16\,384
        pixels to at most 100 components, followed by an SVM; tests whether
        raw pixels can replace hand-crafted features.
  \item \textbf{CNN (images):} the required deep network, which learns its own
        features from the images.
\end{itemize}
Scaling and PCA are part of a scikit-learn \texttt{Pipeline}, so they are
fitted on the training folds only.

\subsubsection{CNN layout}
The CNN (\cref{tab:cnn-layout}) consists of convolution
blocks, each a $3 \times 3$ convolution with ReLU activation followed by
$2 \times 2$ max pooling, then dropout (0.5) and a softmax layer with one output
per gesture. The convolutions use zero padding: a convolution
keeps the size of its input and only the pooling halves it, so after five blocks
the image is exactly $4 \times 4$. Without padding every convolution would also
remove the outer pixels, which hold the start and end of a gesture and the
highest Doppler velocities. ReLU, $\max(0, x)$, does not saturate for positive
inputs, so the gradient does not vanish through the five layers, and it is cheap
to compute; its sparse output also suits the spectrograms, in which most pixels
are zero after noise removal. With 26\,436 parameters the network is small
enough for 960 samples. It is trained with the cross-entropy loss and mini-batch
SGD (batch size 32); training stops when the loss on 10\% of the training data
has not decreased for 10 epochs.

\subsection{Validation Procedure and Performance Indicators}
\label{sec:validation}
Every model is validated in two ways:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Leave-one-subject-out (LOSO):} six folds, each trained on five
        persons and validated on the sixth. This measures recognition of a
        person not seen during training, the aim of the project. The LOSO
        accuracy is the mean over the six persons of the best hyperparameter
        setting; because the same folds choose and score the setting, it is
        slightly optimistic.
  \item \textbf{Random split:} the 80/20 split of
        \cref{fig:pipeline}. The hyperparameters are chosen with 5-fold
        cross-validation on the 768 training samples (validation accuracy); the
        best settings are saved and tested once on the 192
        test samples.
\end{itemize}
The 40 repetitions per person and gesture come from one recording, so in a
random split the same person appears in training and validation. The classes
are balanced, so accuracy is the main metric, together with macro-averaged
precision, recall and F1 and by confusion matrices.

\subsection{Hyperparameter Tuning}
\label{sec:tuning}
All models are tuned with a grid search: every setting is trained on the
training folds and the one with the highest mean validation accuracy is
selected, separately for LOSO and for the random split (\cref{tab:hyper}).
\Cref{fig:tuning} shows the training and validation accuracy along the most
important hyperparameter of the classical models.

\subsubsection{Classical models}
Three observations follow from \cref{fig:tuning}.
First, under LOSO the validation accuracy stays 0.14--0.42 below the training
accuracy for every setting: the limit is the generalisation to a new person, not
the fit. Second, the random split selects more flexible settings than LOSO
(\cref{tab:hyper}): an RBF kernel with $C = 10$ instead of a linear kernel for
the SVM, and fully grown trees for the RF. A random split rewards memorising
person-specific details, because the same person is in the training and
validation folds. Third, LR improves up to $C = 10$ (0.78) and drops slightly
with weaker regularisation (0.76 at $C = 1000$), and SVM + PCA does not exceed
0.60 under LOSO, against 0.94 for the random split.

\subsubsection{CNN layout search}
For the CNN the number of convolution blocks is the
most important hyperparameter. Starting at four blocks, one block of 32 filters
is added at a time until the LOSO validation accuracy goes down
(\cref{fig:cnn-depth}). The learning rate and dropout are fixed at 0.03 and 0.5,
the values chosen in an earlier nested search on the previous pre-processing
(dropout 0.5 in five of six folds, learning rates 0.01--0.1). Four blocks reach
0.67, five 0.73 and six 0.69, so five blocks are selected. The training accuracy
is 0.98--0.99 for every depth, so the depth changes the generalisation, not the
fit. Each block halves the feature map and enlarges the receptive field: with
four blocks a unit sees 46 of the 128 pixels, with five blocks 94, which covers
most of a gesture. A sixth block adds parameters (34\,148 versus 26\,436)
without new context. The random split cannot separate five and six blocks (0.96
for both).

\Cref{fig:comparison} shows the
results: on the random split all models reach 0.92--0.97, but for a new person

\subsubsection{Comparison of the models}
Under LOSO the three feature models are the

lies in the image. Macro-F1 is within 0.01 of the accuracy for every model, as
expected for balanced classes.

outperforms the other image model, but stays below the feature models; more

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}
% =====================================================================
\placeholder{%

This work classified four radar hand gestures from micro-Doppler spectrograms,
with the emphasis on recognising gestures of a person not seen during training.
Five models were compared with leave-one-subject-out (LOSO) validation and a
random 80/20 split. Logistic regression on 27 hand-crafted features generalised
best: a LOSO accuracy of $0.78 \pm 0.13$ (macro-F1 0.78) and 0.95 on the random
test set. The SVM and the random forest on the same features were equally good
within the variation between persons (0.76); the CNN on the spectrogram images
reached 0.73 and an SVM on PCA-reduced images 0.60. Two findings stand out.
First, the random split overestimates the accuracy for a new person by 0.17 to
0.34, because repetitions of the same person are highly correlated, and it
favours more flexible settings. Second, features that describe the motion
(Doppler centroid, envelopes, duration) transfer better to a new person than
image pixels. The main limitation is the small number of persons: the accuracy
ranges from 0.41 to 0.91 between persons. Future work should add persons and
recording sessions and extend the CNN
search to the learning rate and dropout.
