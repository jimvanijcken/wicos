\section*{Summary}
% =====================================================================
% \placeholder{%

% project:
% \begin{itemize}

%   \item Main pre-processing / feature-extraction choices (Task~1).
%   \item Models compared (incl.\ the mandatory deep neural network) and how they
%         were validated (Task~2).
%   \item Key quantitative result(s) on the unseen test data (e.g.\ accuracy,
%         macro-F1) and the selected final model.
%   \item One-sentence main take-away.
% \end{itemize}}

This report classifies four radar hand gestures (wave, pinch, swipe and click)
from the micro-Doppler spectrograms of the DopNet dataset: six persons, 40
samples per gesture and person. A test set of 20\% is separated first. Each
spectrogram is pre-processed separately: the noise is removed with a threshold
10\,dB above the noise level of the far Doppler bins, the Doppler axis is
cropped and the values are scaled to $[0, 1]$. The models use either the
spectrograms resized to $128 \times 128$ pixels or the micro-Doppler envelope
features of Amin et al. Logistic regression, an SVM and a random forest are
trained on the features, and an SVM on principal components and a
convolutional neural network (CNN) on the images. Models and hyperparameters
are chosen with 5-fold cross-validation on the training set, and
leave-one-subject-out (LOSO) shows how they generalise to a new person. A CNN
with five convolution blocks is the final model, with a validation accuracy of
0.967 and a test accuracy of 0.948 (macro-F1 0.948). For a new person its
accuracy drops to 0.74, the level of the feature models, so more persons are
needed to recognise a gesture regardless of who performs it.

\section{Machine Learning Pipeline}
\label{sec:pipeline}
% =====================================================================
The data comes from the DopNet dataset published by the UCL Radar Group \cite{dopnet}. It has been recorded using an Ancortek 24 GHz FMCW radar with a bandwidth of 750 MHz. The recorded samples are micro Doppler spectrograms that show the radar magnitude as a function of time and Doppler frequency. The Doppler frequency is proportional to the radial velocity of the measured moving object. Movement towards the radar generates a positive Doppler shift, while movement away from the radar generates a negative Doppler shift.
\\\\
The used dataset contains 960 labelled samples of different gestures made with a human hand. Each sample is provided as a CSV file, labelled with its gesture, person and repetition. There are 4 gestures (wave, pinch, swipe and click) performed by 6 persons. The measurement for each combination has been repeated 40 times, resulting in a perfectly balanced dataset.
\\\\
Each sample has 800 Doppler bins, where zero Doppler is in the centre at bin 400. The dataset does not contain NaN or infinite values. The magnitude values have already been normalized to each sample's maximum at 0 dB. The goal of this project is to train a model that can classify the 4 gestures based on their spectrograms. The main model will focus on recognising gestures only from people it has seen before. The generalization of the chosen models to new people will also be tested, but that is not the primary objective for this report.

\subsection{Pipeline}
\Cref{fig:pipeline} gives an overview of the pipeline. First, the 960 samples are split into a training set of 768 samples and a test set of 192 samples (\cref{sec:split}). Every spectrogram is then pre-processed separately through noise removal, cropping and scaling (\cref{sec:task1}).
\\\\
The completely pre-processed spectrograms are used for the image models: a convolution neural network (CNN) and an SVM on principal components (SVM + PCA). The spectrograms without noise are used to compute envelope features using the paper by Amin et al (\cref{sec:features}). In total 760 features are computed. The set of features a model uses (3, 44 or 716) is treated as a hyperparameter. Standardization and PCA are fitted on the training folds only. 
\\\\
The hyperparameters of every model are tuned with a grid search on stratified 5-fold cross-validation on the training set. For every model the setting with the highest mean validation accuracy is chosen, and the final model is the model with the highest validation accuracy overall. These settings are then fixed. Leave-one-subject-out (LOSO) on the training set shows how each model generalises to a new person. Finally, every model is retrained on all 768 training samples and evaluated once on the test set. The models are compared on accuracy, macro-averaged F1 score and confusion matrices (\cref{sec:task2}).

 Yet, the spectrograms have varying magnitude ranges depending on both the gesture and the person. Most of the values however contain background noise instead of gesture energy. The data needs to be made more uniform by removing noise and rescaling such that the model is prevented from learning a gesture mainly based on the background noise level. This also poses a challenge generalizing the model to more people since the model will learn person specific features with just 6 people in the training data. The gestures should be distinguished on the distribution of gesture energy over time and doppler bins.

\subsection{Pre-processing}
Since there are no invalid values in the provided dataset, no samples have to be removed. All samples will be processed in four steps: noise removal, cropping, scaling and resizing. The goal of the pre-processing is to turn the raw data into clean spectrograms of uniform dimensions, that can be used for feature extraction and model training.

\paragraph{Noise removal}
As shown before in \autoref{fig:doppler_profile_persons}, the samples all have their own noise floor level. This background noise can be removed since it contains no useful information about the gesture. Because of the sample specific noise floor, the noise removal process also has to be sample specific. Clipping below a fixed threshold for example would cut into the gesture of some samples, while leaving noise in others. 
\\\\
The gesture mainly takes place in the bins close to zero Doppler, while the bins far away from the centre mainly consist of noise. \autoref{fig:doppler_profile_persons} shows the median levels off to a flat line. By inspection of that same figure, the outer 60 bins on each side are taken as the far bins. The median of the far bin pixels is then defined as the spectrogram's noise level. The median is well suited for this, rather than the mean, since it is less sensitive to a few bins that do contain the gesture (like the broadband stripes of the click gesture). As long as more than half of the far-bin pixels contain noise, the median gives a good estimate of the noise level.
\\\\
% \todo{add plot to show that it doesnt really matter what far bins we take}
Thresholding at the median would still pass half of the noise samples. Assuming complex Gaussian noise, the power per pixel $P$ is exponentially distributed with mean noise power $\mu$, so the probability that noise exceeds a threshold $p$ is 
\begin{equation}
  P_{\mathrm{fa}} = \Pr(P > p) = e^{-p/\mu}
  \;\Rightarrow\; p = \mu \ln(1/P_{\mathrm{fa}}),
\end{equation}
with the median at $p_{50} = \mu \ln 2$. The margin above the median is then independent of the unknown noise power and set only by the chosen false-alarm probability:
\begin{equation}
  \Delta_{\mathrm{dB}}
  = 10 \log_{10}\!\left(\frac{p}{p_{50}}\right)
  = 10 \log_{10}\!\left(\frac{\ln(1/P_{\mathrm{fa}})}{\ln 2}\right).
\end{equation}
As seen in \autoref{fig:noise_margin}, the far-bin pixels can indeed be modelled as Gaussian noise. The tail above 10 dB is explained by the broadband stripes in the click gesture. With $P_{\mathrm{fa}} = 10^{-3}$, i.e.\ the threshold at the 99.9th percentile of the noise, the margin becomes
\begin{equation}
  \Delta_{\mathrm{dB}}
  = 10 \log_{10}\!\left(\frac{\ln 1000}{\ln 2}\right)
  \approx 10.0~\text{dB}.
\end{equation}
This 99.9\% margin is a trade-off between removing more noise and preserving gesture information. For each sample, all values are clipped at the sample specific noise floor plus that fixed 10 dB margin.

\paragraph{Cropping}
Since the bins far from zero Doppler contain mainly noise and hold little gesture information, they might as well be removed to improve the Doppler resolution when resizing the spectrogram later on. For each Doppler bin, the pixels of all training samples are taken together, and the share that lies above the sample-specific noise threshold is computed (\autoref{fig:crop_rule}). This share decreases steadily away from zero Doppler. A bin is removed if less than 1\% of its pixels exceed the threshold. With a symmetric crop around zero Doppler, this keeps bins 90--709 (620 bins). A narrower crop would give a higher resolution after resizing, at the cost of removing the tails of gestures that do reach into the far bins. 

\paragraph{Scaling}
After the noise removal, each sample still has a different noise floor. The model should not learn the gestures based on this noise floor. The sample dependent noise floor should therefore be converted to a uniform noise floor. This is done using per sample scaling. The noise threshold (median + margin) becomes zero, since it has no information and the maximum (0 dB) becomes 1.

\paragraph{Resizing}
Finally, the spectrograms have to be made uniform in their dimensions so that they can be used as input for a CNN. A resolution of 128 x 128 is chosen as the output size. The spectrograms are all resized using linear interpolation. This does remove gesture duration, which did hold relevant information so that will be added as a separate feature later on.

\subsection{Feature Extraction and Selection}
\label{sec:features}
\noindent
The CNN will learn features from the pre-processed and resized spectrograms. The classical models on the other hand would have to process $128^2=16384$ pixels, which is a very large number compared to the just 768 available training samples. Resizing also removed the gesture duration. Instead, the feature models will be given a compact representation of the spectrograms based on their micro-Doppler envelopes by Amin et al \cite{amin2019}. All envelope features are computed on the spectrograms after noise has been removed but before and cropping, scaling and resizing so the original duration is preserved. 

\subsubsection{Envelope extraction}
\label{sec:envelopes}

The envelopes are computed on the gesture's linear power above the sample's own noise floor, $P(d, n)$.The noise is removed like in \autoref{sec:task1}, so that pure noise has power zero. Here $d$ is the Doppler bin relative to zero Doppler (positive is motion towards the radar) and $n$ the time bin. The spectrograms are then cropped in time to just the gesture span: from the first to the last time bin with at least 5\% of the power of the strongest time bin.

\paragraph{Effective bandwidth}
The spectrogram is split into a positive and a negative Doppler half. For each half the gesture power is summed over time and, counting outward from zero Doppler, the effective bandwidth ends where 99.9\% of the energy of that half is reached. This Doppler bin is the maximum positive Doppler $f_p$ or maximum negative Doppler $f_n$ of the gesture. A fixed energy share is used as the effective bandwidth. A share of just 99\% was found to cut the outer lobes of the gesture, while 99.9\% keeps them. Isolated noise cells far from zero Doppler carry almost no energy and stay outside.

\paragraph{Energy and threshold}
In every time bin the energy of each half is
\begin{equation}
  E_U(n) = \sum_{d > 0} P(d, n), \qquad E_L(n) = \sum_{d < 0} P(d, n),
\end{equation}
and the threshold is a fixed fraction of it, $T_U(n) = \sigma_U E_U(n)$ and $T_L(n) = \sigma_L E_L(n)$ with $\sigma < 1$. As in the paper, $\sigma$ is chosen per sample and per half such that the threshold lies exactly at the maximum Doppler in the time bin $n_p$ where that Doppler bin is strongest:
\begin{equation}
  \sigma_U = \frac{P(f_p, n_p)}{E_U(n_p)}, \qquad n_p = \arg\max_n P(f_p, n),
\end{equation}
and likewise $\sigma_L$ with $f_n$. In every other time bin the threshold scales with the energy of that time bin.

\paragraph{Envelope}
The envelope is the outermost Doppler bin that still reaches the threshold:
\begin{equation}
  e_U(n) = \max\{d > 0 : P(d, n) \geq T_U(n)\}, \qquad
  e_L(n) = \min\{d < 0 : P(d, n) \geq T_L(n)\}.
\end{equation}
Time bins without energy in a half get envelope 0.
\paragraph{Feature vector}
As in the paper, the envelopes are concatenated into one feature vector, $e = [e_U, e_L]$. Each envelope is placed in a fixed window of 358 time bins, starting at the gesture start and zero-padded after the gesture end. 99\% of the training gesture spans fit in this window; the longer samples are cut. Nothing is resampled, so the gesture duration remains visible in the feature vector.

\label{sec:feature_list}
From the envelopes, five feature groups are computed for every sample. \autoref{tab:features} shows these groups: the first two groups are the features as in the paper by Amin et al., the other three are compact representations of the envelopes for the classical models.

  \label{tab:features}

\noindent
The window envelopes keep the full time resolution but with a length of 716 values they are large compared to the number of training samples. Additionally, envelopes of the same gesture performed at different speeds are misaligned. The time-normalized feature set solves this averaging each envelope over 16 equal segments of the gesture span. The gesture duration is kept separately as length $T$. This also dampens single bin jumps caused by noise. The 90th percentile similarly acts as a robust maximum less sensitive to noise.

\section{Task 2: Model Development, Validation and Comparison}
\label{sec:task2}

\subsection{Model Selection and Design}
\label{sec:models}
Five models are trained on the pre-processed data: three on the envelope
features (\autoref{tab:features}) and two on the $128 \times 128$ images.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Logistic regression (LR):} multinomial model with the
        cross-entropy loss and an L2 penalty; the linear, interpretable baseline.
  \item \textbf{SVM:} soft-margin support vector machine with a linear or RBF
        kernel.
  \item \textbf{Random forest (RF):} decision trees on bootstrap samples with a
        random subset of features per split, combined by majority vote.
  \item \textbf{SVM + PCA:} PCA reduces the 16\,384 pixels, followed
        by an SVM.
  \item \textbf{CNN:} the deep network, which learns its own features
        from the images.
\end{itemize}
The feature models choose between three feature sets: the three empirical
features (\emph{empirical}), the empirical features with the time-normalised
envelopes, the envelope statistics and the recording length (\emph{compact}, 44
features) and the window envelopes (\emph{window}, 716 features). Scaling and
PCA are part of a scikit-learn \texttt{Pipeline}, so they are fitted on the
training folds only.

\subsubsection{CNN layout}
The CNN (\autoref{tab:cnn-layout}) stacks blocks of a $3 \times 3$ convolution
with ReLU and $2 \times 2$ max pooling, followed by dropout and a softmax layer.
The convolutions use zero padding, so only the pooling halves the image and the
outer pixels, which hold the start and end of a gesture and the highest Doppler
velocities, are not lost. ReLU does not saturate for positive inputs, so the
gradient does not vanish through the five layers and its sparse output suits
the images, in which most pixels are zero after the noise removal. The network
is trained with the cross-entropy loss and mini-batch SGD (batch size 32);
training stops when the loss on 10\% of the training data has not decreased for
10 epochs.

\subsection{Validation Procedure and Performance Indicators}
\label{sec:validation}
The models follow the pipeline of \autoref{fig:pipeline} and the split of
\autoref{fig:split-folds}:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The model and its hyperparameters are chosen with stratified 5-fold
        cross-validation on the 768 training samples (validation accuracy);
        the final model is the model with the highest mean validation accuracy.
  \item The chosen setting of every model is evaluated with leave-one-subject-out
        (LOSO) on the training samples, to see how it generalises to a new person.
  \item Every chosen setting is retrained on the entire training set and
        evaluated once on the 192 test samples, after the final model is chosen.
\end{enumerate}
The classes are balanced, so accuracy is the main metric, together with the
macro-averaged precision, recall and F1 score and a confusion matrix. Scores
are given as mean $\pm$ standard deviation over the folds.

\subsection{Hyperparameter Tuning}
\label{sec:tuning}
Every model is tuned with a grid search on 5-fold cross-validation
(\autoref{tab:hyper}); the setting with the highest mean validation accuracy is

\subsubsection{Classical models}
The compact feature set is chosen for all three feature models
(\autoref{fig:tuning}). The three empirical features alone reach at most 0.76:
they describe the size of a gesture, not its course in time. The window
envelopes, 716 values for 768 samples, overfit: for LR the training accuracy
rises to 1.0 with weaker regularisation while the validation accuracy falls from
0.71 to 0.61. Grids were extended where the chosen value lay at their edge: every SVM
$C \geq 100$ gives 0.887 (the training data is fitted perfectly), RF depth 20 equals fully
grown trees, 1000 trees are worse than 300 (0.908), and for SVM + PCA 20
components are best (0.87 with 5, 0.90 with 10, 0.93 with 50, 0.91 with 100).

\subsubsection{CNN layout search}
The number of convolution blocks is chosen first, with the learning rate and
dropout fixed at 0.03 and 0.5: starting at four blocks, a block of 32 filters is
added until the validation accuracy goes down (\autoref{fig:cnn}a). Four blocks
reach 0.939, five 0.964 and six 0.961, so five blocks are chosen. The training
accuracy is 0.99 for every depth, so the depth changes the generalisation, not
the fit. Each block halves the feature map and enlarges the receptive field: with
four blocks a unit sees 46 of the 128 pixels, with five blocks 94, which covers
most of a gesture, while a sixth block only adds parameters. For five blocks,
five of the six learning rate and dropout settings reach 0.96--0.97
(\autoref{fig:cnn}b); only learning rate 0.1 with dropout 0.5 is unstable
(0.866). Learning rate 0.03 with dropout 0.25 is chosen (0.967).

\subsubsection{Model choice}
The final model has the highest mean validation accuracy, chosen before the
test set is used: the CNN ($0.967 \pm 0.017$, \autoref{tab:results}). Its lead
over SVM + PCA ($0.949 \pm 0.021$) is one standard deviation, but on the same
folds the CNN is better than SVM + PCA and the RF in four of the five folds
(equal to the RF in the fifth) and better than LR and SVM in all five. LOSO
supports the choice: for a new person the CNN equals the best feature model and
beats SVM + PCA. The RF is the alternative if interpretability or training cost
matters (equal under LOSO, 0.05 lower in validation).

\subsubsection{Generalisation to a new person}
Under LOSO every model loses accuracy (\autoref{tab:results}): the CNN drops from
0.967 to 0.737, SVM + PCA from 0.949 to 0.612 and the feature models to
0.73--0.75. The cross-validation folds contain the same persons as the training
folds and therefore reward person-specific details. For a new person the RF,
CNN, LR and SVM lie within 0.02, much less than the spread between persons
(standard deviation 0.06--0.12); SVM + PCA is clearly the worst, because its
components describe where the energy lies in the images of the training
persons. Person A is the hardest for most models (\autoref{fig:results}b).

\subsubsection{Test set}
The early-stopping loss of the final CNN follows the training loss without
rising and is lowest at epoch 39, whose weights are restored
(\autoref{fig:results}a).
The test accuracies lie within 0.03 of the validation accuracies: the test set
contains the same persons as the training set, so it confirms the
cross-validation estimate, not the generalisation to a new person. The CNN
reaches 0.948 (macro-F1 0.948). Of its 10 errors, 4 are wave versus swipe and 4
are a swipe or click predicted as pinch (\autoref{fig:results}c): the long hand
movements and the short finger movements. Precision, recall and F1 lie within
0.01 of the accuracy for every model, as expected with balanced classes.

\subsubsection{Summary}
The CNN is the final model: it has the best validation accuracy and reaches
0.948 on the test set. For a new person all models lose 0.12--0.34 accuracy and
the CNN and the feature models end up at the same level (0.73--0.75). In short, more
persons are needed to close this gap and to obtain higher accuracies.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}
% =====================================================================

This work classified four radar hand gestures from micro-Doppler spectrograms.
The noise was removed per recording, the Doppler axis cropped and the envelopes
of Amin et al.\ extracted. Five models were chosen with 5-fold cross-validation
on the training set, evaluated with leave-one-subject-out (LOSO) and tested once
on a held-out test set. A CNN with five convolution blocks on the pre-processed
images was the best model: a validation accuracy of $0.967 \pm 0.017$ and a test
accuracy of 0.948 (macro-F1 0.948). Among the feature models the random forest
on the compact envelope features was best (0.915). The main limitation is the
generalisation to a new person: under LOSO all models lose 0.12--0.34 accuracy,
and the CNN (0.74) is not better than the feature models (0.73--0.75). The
recordings of one person are highly correlated, and six persons are too few to
learn the variation between persons. Future work should add more persons and
more recording sessions, vary the gesture speed and timing, and reduce the jumps
of the envelopes in weak time bins.
